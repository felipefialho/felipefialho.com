[what Front-end Developers need to know](/en/blog/what-front-end-developers-need-to-know/)
and another one about
[15 things I would do if I were starting my career](/en/blog/15-things-i-would-do-if-i-were-starting-my-developer-career/).

Now I'm going to write a kind of practical guide with a few paths for people who
are starting out professionally (or not).

It's important to say that my main role is Front-end Developer, so I'm more
plugged into that market and naturally the tips are biased toward Front-end
Development, but they can be adapted to pretty much any area of programming.

I also want to stress that people are different and no journey is going to be
the same, so don't take these tips as absolute truth. They're just a collection
of things that worked for me and for other people I know, but they may or may
not work for you.

## Studying

![Learning Culture](assets/2019-10-30-learning-culture.gif)_Credit:
[fulcrumlabs.ai](https://www.fulcrumlabs.ai/blog/introduction-learning-culture/)_

No secret here, studying is going to be a constant throughout your whole career.
Learning concepts, theories, ways of working, programming languages, and
reinforcing all of that from time to time is essential.

There are two possible paths, and they're not mutually exclusive: formal
education and informal education.

### Formal education

There's a lot of debate about whether or not you need formal education in
programming-related fields. It's a very individual choice. I know amazing devs
who went to college and badass people who don't have a degree, and my only
certainty is that it's **not** a decisive factor for a successful career.

Fortunately, more and more companies are dropping the degree requirement, and in
my opinion that makes the market more interesting and dynamic. Even so, having a
degree is still important, for example, to get a work visa in some countries.

As for learning, I believe it can be worthwhile for establishing and learning
more academic concepts about design, programming logic, software engineering,
computer science, and so on. Part of that knowledge is going to be super useful
over time.

Some examples of degrees are:

- Software Engineering
- Computer Engineering
- Computer Science

Design-oriented programs, like Digital Design and similar, can also be good
choices, especially if you want to go deep on topics like user experience and
interface development.

Another interesting option is technologist programs (short vocational degrees in
Brazil). They're shorter, usually 2 to 3 years. Some of them are also accepted
by companies that require a degree, or for getting a visa in some countries.

As a reference, check out this talk by
[Afonso Pacifer](https://twitter.com/afonsopacifer) at Front in Sampa, which for
me was one of the best takes on this topic in recent times.

### Informal education (aka street JavaScript)

The term **street JavaScript** (which I took from the video above) fits informal
education like a glove. And it doesn't matter whether or not you have a degree,
you're going to need a lot of informal study.

That's because what's taught in universities is not always aligned (and almost
never is 😶) with the increasingly dynamic job market we're living in. So a very
well-known phenomenon happens:

> People graduate but aren't prepared to face day-to-day challenges

The upside is that thousands of pieces of content, both practical and
theoretical, have been produced in recent years, and better yet, a good chunk of
them are free or reasonably priced.

You can get informal education in several ways:

- Taking courses on
  [Udemy](https://click.linksynergy.com/fs-bin/click?id=16ht5juwidA&offerid=679157.10&type=3&subid=0&LSNSUBSITE=LSNSUBSITE),
  [Alura](https://www.alura.com.br/), etc
- Looking up definitions and references on [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org)
- Doing exercises on [w3schools](https://www.w3schools.com/)
- Reading [Blogs](https://github.com/felipefialho/frontend-feed)
- Watching videos on [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com)
- Following issues on [FrontendBR](https://github.com/frontendbr/forum) 😋
- Asking and answering questions on [Stack Overflow](https://stackoverflow.com/)
- Etc

There are also some platforms that make studying more playful, a few examples
are:

- [HackerRank](https://www.hackerrank.com/)
- [CodeSignal](https://codesignal.com/developers/)
- [CodeWars](https://www.codewars.com/)
- [Codility](https://app.codility.com/programmers/)

The upside is tracking your progress (which is even visible to some companies)
and being able to study by practicing, which makes the learning process easier.

And speaking of practicing...

A few years ago I wrote an
[article worth reading later](/blog/aproximando-seus-estudos-de-cenarios-reais/) (in Portuguese)
about how to bring theory closer to practice. Now I'm going to go deeper on that
and give new examples and tips.

There are many ways to practice, and I'll list some of them. With them you won't
only learn, you'll also build a kind of portfolio that can help a lot in landing
new opportunities.

### Create experiments

It's a fun and extremely effective way to practice, because you not only
reinforce what you're studying, you also develop your creativity.

Let me give you an example. In the past there was a _boom_ of new stuff in CSS,
a lot of new properties showed up. In 2013 I was loving drawing with CSS and
studying JavaScript... and I had always wanted to learn piano, so why not try to
build a piano with CSS and JavaScript?

This was the result:

- [Piano Keyboard - To play piano using CSS and JavaScript 😎](https://piano.felipefialho.com/)

[Codepen](https://codepen.io), for example, is a fantastic place to create
experiments like this. Just by browsing around you'll find amazing ideas that
can inspire you to create your own projects.

Throughout my career I've done plenty of experiments like this, and you can
check some of them on the [lab](/lab) page of this site.

It's an incredible way to learn by playing.

### Build your own site (and/or blog)

Every time I feel like learning (and practicing) new technologies, I rewrite all
the code of my site using a new stack.

With this tactic, besides learning new technologies, you also learn new
methodologies, concepts, and project architecture. And there's more: the work of
thinking about the layout and the user experience is a phenomenal exercise in
itself.

The result, besides the learning, is your personal brand available on the
internet. Your site can be (and for sure will be) a great business card for your
work. It can say something about your code, your skills, and a bit about how you
think.

This is already the third version of my site, and I've already
[written an article](/blog/como-foi-desenvolver-meu-novo-blog-usando-o-gatsbyjs/) (in Portuguese)
about those experiences, explaining all the technologies and concepts I applied
here. It's worth reading to discover all the possibilities that can come out of
a personal project.

### Open pull requests

Opening pull requests on other people's repositories is another really cool way
to practice. That's because this time you're not the one deciding which stack or
conventions will be used, so you get exposed to new ways of working.

![Bootstrap](assets/1_ukSvk4hGI4nw4sEhte7kyQ.png)

The image above is a screenshot of one of the
[first PRs](https://github.com/twbs/bootstrap/pull/11162) I opened, none other
than on Bootstrap in mid-2013. Besides being super cool to help a project I
actually used, I got some really nice feedback on the code and on the quality of
the PR.

It's interesting to note that by opening Pull Requests you can also develop some
soft skills, such as:

- Teamwork
- Flexibility
- Written communication
- Among others

Even your English can get some practice, since a good part of the documentation
and code is written in that language.

Besides contributing to open projects that can help lots of people, which is
already pretty motivating, this work also stays available on your Github (or
similar) for other people and companies to evaluate.

### Recreate famous libs

In the so-called _real world_ of the job market, it's not always a good idea to
_reinvent the wheel_. There are thousands of solutions that address many of the
problems in modern Software Development.

But during your studies you have total freedom to build whatever you see fit.

Trying to recreate or reproduce famous libs, or the concepts they apply, is
another interesting way to practice.

You'll understand how things work under the hood and improve your skills in the
technologies you chose.

And who knows, maybe the wheel you built ends up better than the original wheel?
😜

### Rebuild pages from famous sites and apps

Especially if your focus is Front-end Development, another way to practice is to
list a bunch of sites and apps that you use (or like) and try to replicate their
layouts.

That way you mainly develop your CSS skills (since you need to replicate real
layouts), plus HTML and JavaScript at the interface level.

You don't need to rebuild the whole project, but it's worth picking the pages
and components you like the most and trying to get close to the original (or
maybe even improve the layout and suggest it to the company in question 😂).

### Challenge yourself with real company tests on frontend-challenges

This tip is a killer.

A few years ago, I created a repository to collect real (open source) tests from
companies in Brazil and around the world.

The name of this repository is:

- [frontend-challenges](https://github.com/felipefialho/frontend-challenges)

What does that mean?

You'll literally challenge your skills on the tests companies use for hiring.
Theoretically, if you manage to complete the challenge, you have the technical
ability to get approved in a hiring process.

More than that, companies are looking for developers all the time, and if you do
a really good test there's a real chance that company will reach out to chat
with you.

And even if that doesn't happen, you can ask for feedback on the quality of your
test, which is very valuable and can help you fix several aspects of your code
or your line of reasoning.

### First of all: LinkedIn

A long time ago, in a land far away, people used printed paper with a summary of
their qualifications and skills. That paper was called a Curriculum Vitae (CV).

With the digital age, that paper was replaced by a digital version, usually
attached to an email. Time went by and I consider the CV totally unnecessary for
digital professions. Most of that information can be found on professional
sites like LinkedIn.

That's exactly why it's important to have an updated LinkedIn profile. It's a
way to have your professional background available in a format people are
already used to.

If your profile is outdated, take a little time to add more recent information
about yourself. It can help a lot in hiring processes.

One tip: if you're in a hiring process that requires sending a CV as an
attachment, LinkedIn itself has a tool to generate CVs in PDF format based on
the information in your profile.

![Download CV - LinkedIn](assets/2019-10-30-download-cv.png)

### After all: Github

Part of the practical tips I gave make use of [Github](https://github.com), and
you probably already have a profile there, or at least you've heard of it.

Opinions are divided, but these days I also think it's very important to have an
updated and active Github profile.

Speaking for myself, I would never refuse to hire or even contact someone for
not having open code available. Amazing people would be discarded because of
that.

But when a person has activity on open source code, you can get some sense of
their technical level. In some cases you can skip practical tests, and even
evaluate some soft skills based on their engagement in projects.

Besides, Github is a social network like any other.

## Get prepared

Got a positive response for that job you liked so much? Time to get prepared!

Preparation is essential in any field, including development and programming.

### Research the company

Knowing more about the company is super important, not only things about its
business, but also understanding, for example, what the work environment is
like there and reading the opinions of people who work (and used to work) there.

It's worth checking the company's website and searching for news online and on
social networks.

To see what people think of the company, you can talk to them directly on
social networks like LinkedIn or check what was published on sites like
[Glassdoor](https://www.glassdoor.com.br).

### Interview the company

Hiring processes are a two-way street, so never skip interviewing the company
you're talking to.

Clear up all your doubts, ask things like:

- What technologies do you use?
- What are the expected day-to-day tasks?
- What's the career path?
- How does the company plan to grow?
- Where does it see itself in a while?
- How's the revenue?
- How does it make a profit?
- What's its competitive advantage?
- What are the working hours?
- How flexible are the hours?
- Is there a _dress code_?

There's a really nice list with lots of interesting questions
[in this repository](https://github.com/viraptor/reverse-interview/).

If the job is on-site, you'll spend most of your day in that environment. Even
if the job is _home office_, you'll interact with the company's processes and
people most of the day.

So don't skip clearing up **all** your doubts, and make absolutely sure you feel
comfortable by the end of the interview.

### Develop soft skills

The days of developers who only coded are fading (thank God). We hear more and
more about soft skills in tech environments.

Your participation on several fronts of the business is essential for the
company's growth and for your professional and personal development.

Things like:

- Communication
- Teamwork
- Leadership
- Professionalism
- Flexibility
- Creativity
- Proactivity
- Empathy
- Etc

These skills are essential for any professional, and they'll be evaluated in
hiring processes and in your day-to-day as a dev. And yes, they can really
affect your professional success.

The good news is that they're skills like any other, so even if you struggle
with some of them at first, all of them can be improved and developed.

### Do well on the test

Practically every company has tests to evaluate people's level before deciding
on a hire. This is important even to align expectations on role and salary.

The tests can be of many kinds:

- Questionnaire
- Practical tests
- Theoretical tests
- Assisted test (pair programming style)
- Group dynamics

It's actually not uncommon for more than one type of test to be applied in the
hiring process.

During the tests, the tip is to always pay attention to what is being asked.
Some tests may try to lead you into a mistake, precisely to test your analytical
skills.

If the company doesn't make all the phases of the process clear, sites like
[Glassdoor](https://www.glassdoor.com.br) can help you understand all the
stages and which tests will be applied.

And again, repositories like
[frontend-challenges](https://github.com/felipefialho/frontend-challenges) can
be an incredible ally at this stage, as I mentioned earlier.

### Don't give up❗️

We're in a hurry, a big hurry, but a career is built over many years and,
believe me, it's going to have ups and downs. That's natural. Every moment of
sadness and joy matters for your growth as a person, which will also impact your
professional growth.

So if you don't do well in your first hiring processes or you don't get
selected, don't get discouraged!

Try to understand where you didn't do well and always ask for feedback from the
people who interviewed you.

Knowing how to move forward and learn from mistakes is essential for building a
solid career.

Every step, no matter how small, matters in your growth. But they're your steps

We all carry our own universes inside us, and nobody else in the world will have

position than you, don't use them as a benchmark to judge your own progress.
That can really do a lot of harm.

So yes, have people as inspirations and examples, that can help you build your
own paths, but always compare yourself only with yourself.

I talk a lot about open source. But take it easy.

Despite all the [amazing philosophy](/en/blog/how-to-contribute-to-open-source/)
around open source and dev communities, you need to take care of yourself first.

What do I mean by that?

Communities are addictive, and it's super common to take on lots of
responsibilities when we first get involved.

We want to create content, give a thousand talks, write a bunch of posts, and
organize events. Many times, we want to do all of that before even having a job,
having professional stability, or even taking care of our mental health.

That's why I suggest focusing first on your professional and personal
development. That way you'll be well enough to maybe help other people with your
work.
