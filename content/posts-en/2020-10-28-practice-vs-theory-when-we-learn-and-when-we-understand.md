## Starting with the differences

### When do we understand?

Understanding lives in the realm of ideas and theories.

It happens on an intellectual, rational level. It's understanding the mechanism,
the logic or the reason why things exist or work.

When I publish content, for example, I don't expect people to learn, I expect
people to understand the ideas being presented. And I also hope that this
content encourages them to dive deeper into the topics on their own, so that
then they can learn.

### When do we learn?

That's because learning lives in the realm of experience and practice.

It necessarily involves experimentation and repetition. It's practicing and
repeating a given action many times so our brain records that information and so
we can use that learning to connect everything we study with problems or
situations that happen in our lives.

You can even learn the process of learning itself.

The more things we learn and the more times we go through the process of
learning new skills, the more we improve and optimize the way we study and
learn.

You could say we understand theory, but we only truly learn through practice.

## Example: Soccer

For example, you can spend a lot of time studying all the theory of a sport like
soccer.

You can read thousands of texts, watch talks about the latest soccer trends and
even buy some course like:

> "Learn soccer in just 7 days and play for the best clubs in the world"

But you still won't learn. The most you'll get is understanding the mechanism
and the logic of how a soccer match works.

You'll only learn about soccer in two ways: playing or watching as many games as
you can.

That way everything you had understood in theory will make sense in practice,
like the rules, tactics and techniques used during the games.

After playing many times or watching thousands of matches, you'll actually learn
and not just understand, and then the whole mechanism around soccer will feel
natural to you.

## Another example: My videos

As we move forward in our careers, some non-technical skills like communication
become more and more important.

Right now I work as a Tech Lead at
[Juntos Somos Mais](https://www.linkedin.com/company/juntos-somos-mais/) and
it's important that I can communicate with the people on the team as clearly as
possible.

I don't think I communicate badly, but I see plenty of room for improvement. So
I could, for example, spend a good amount of time reading several books and
looking up information on the Internet about how to communicate better. That
would make me understand communication theories, but in practice it wouldn't
help me much.

So what I did was
[create a YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/@felipefialhodev), where I
have to be as clear as possible in my communication or the people watching the
videos simply won't understand.

As a bonus, I also get interesting feedback from the people who follow the
channel, which helps me improve video after video and naturally shows up in my
day to day.

This way I can bring this practical learning into my personal and professional
life.

## One more example: New technologies

I use this same strategy when I want to learn new technologies.

For example, I spent some time studying React frameworks like NextJS and Gatsby,
so I was able to understand how these technologies worked and what kind of
problems they solved, but I hadn't really learned how to work with them yet.

So the solution to actually learn was to rebuild my website using Gatsby (and
you can
[read about how that experience went in this article](/blog/como-foi-desenvolver-meu-novo-blog-usando-o-gatsbyjs/) (in Portuguese)).

I also took the chance to learn other technologies I was studying and already
understood how they worked, like Styled Components and GraphQL.

That's why I think it's very important to look for different ways to practice,
and that was my goal when I compiled open company challenges in
[Front-end Challenges](https://github.com/felipefialho/frontend-challenges).

And it's also why I keep hammering on creating personal projects to practice the
things you're studying.

## Alternating between understanding and learning

Both understanding something new and learning new skills take time, but the time
needed to learn something is significantly greater, since, as I said before,
learning is only possible with lots of repetition and lots of practice.

Of course I'd like to learn as many things as possible, but unfortunately (or
fortunately)
[our time is finite and limited](/blog/2015-09-28-uma-reflexao-sobre-salarios-valor-hora-e-qualidade-de-vida) (in Portuguese).

So a strategy I usually adopt is alternating between **understanding** and
**learning**.

I try to understand things superficially, keeping as many of them as possible on
my radar. I study them, understand how they work and in which situations they
could be used.

As new needs come up in my day to day, I check whether any of those things I
left on the radar could help me solve these new problems.

This way I invest my time only in learning the things that really prove to be
most important day to day. On the other hand, I always stay up to date, since I
follow the news superficially and try to understand how as many things as
possible work.

Making this association of when it's worth using the latest market novelties in
your day to day is only possible when we spend time understanding different
subjects that aren't part of our repertoire yet.

This applies both to things like new languages or frameworks and to
non-technical things:

> The bigger our repertoire, the more connections we'll be able to make

If we stay stuck on the same old topics and solutions, we'll be limited in the
possibilities and the number of solutions we can come up with to solve the
challenges that show up.

In other words, we stagnate (and get worse).
