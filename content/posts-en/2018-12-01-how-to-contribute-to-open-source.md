## How does an open source project work?

Open source, in short, is a project created and maintained by the people who use
it. Say you have an open source soccer field near your house: one person helps
mow the grass, another helps paint the lines, another helps water it, someone
else brings the ball, and everybody gets to play! It's a collaborative system of
growth.

Contributing to open source can be a rewarding way to learn, teach and build
experience in pretty much any skill you can imagine.

On top of that, you get to interact with real industry professionals and ask
them questions, get code reviews and get tips to improve.

I recommend reading the [Open Source Guides](https://opensource.guide/pt) site
to get a deeper understanding of the open source initiative.

_Contribution by [@iaurg](https://github.com/iaurg)_

### And why contribute?

Contribution is what made the development world evolve so much in recent years.

Through these contributions, ideas are born, projects are developed, libs are
built, technologies evolve and, most importantly:

> People are impacted, sharing knowledge and keeping this cycle going.

You can feel the effect even in the job market: with more skilled people
connecting with each other, the profession tends to become more mature,
important and valued. That leads to better conditions, benefits and salaries.

## And yes, companies benefit too

It's not only devs who take advantage of open source initiatives. Like I said
before, the impact can be huge, and companies that work with products and other
digital fronts end up benefiting from it too.

That's because they don't need to reinvent the wheel every time they start a new
project or add a feature to their products. They benefit from libs and knowledge
created before, which saves a lot of development time.

They also benefit because they end up using more robust projects, which tend to
have fewer bugs and have the support of a huge community all over the world.

That's why I think every company that uses open source in its day to day (and
you could probably say that's almost all of them) not only can but **should**
think about ways to give back to the development community whenever possible.

## Some GitHub tips

The tips below can be used on any similar network.

### 1. Search before starting a project

There's a good chance one or more initiatives with the same idea you had already
exist.

So consider whether it's worth contributing to one of those existing projects
instead.

Having fewer projects, but robust and tested ones, is better than having
thousands of duplicate projects that don't create much value.

### 2. Don't be afraid to help with documentation

Documentation is one of the most important parts of a project. It's what guides
the user, who in this case is the developer who's going to use the project.

Despite that importance, it's often undervalued, and you'd help any project with
review, fixes or even translation of the text.

And the best part: you can do it straight from GitHub, without opening a single
line of code.

### 3. Open issues

If you find bugs or have suggestions, open an issue (search first so you don't
create a duplicate).

This helps the maintainers map out the improvements and fixes they need to make.

### 4. Open pull requests

Opening issues is important, but go further. Try to solve it.

That helps not only the project, it also becomes a great source of study.

To this day I can't forget this pull request of mine that got merged into
Bootstrap, written in some truly bizarre English 😍

![](assets/1_ukSvk4hGI4nw4sEhte7kyQ.png)

### 5. Follow the project's standards

Whether it's for issues, pull requests, code or documentation.

If there's a standard, it must be respected. It may seem annoying, but it's
extremely important for the organization and maintenance of the project.

As a bonus, you'll be exposed to new ways of structuring and developing.

### 6. Don't be afraid of feedback

Whenever you take part in any project with other people or write open code, you
may get criticism.

If you get constructive feedback, take it in a positive way. Try to absorb the
information and improve on the points mentioned.

That will make you grow, both professionally and personally.

### 7. Be responsible

Unfortunately it's common to see people get excited about a project, commit to
tasks, and then disappear when it's time to build.

And this happens in every area of open source, from people who offer to help at
events to people who commit to pull requests.

The result is that the people who are actually doing the work have to work twice
as hard to get it delivered.

Don't be that person. If you committed to a task, do it.

That's called responsibility.

### 8. Build to learn

Building projects just to learn, recreating libs to study them, creating things
to challenge yourself is extremely important throughout a career.

Don't let your curiosity and interest fade over time.

That's what makes things so cool in our profession and makes amazing people and
projects show up all the time.

Another point: programming languages are like any other skill, so the best way
to learn and get better is: practicing.

Open source projects can help a lot in putting into practice the things you're
studying day to day.
