## Introduction

Life is a breath and time rolls by like a steamroller. It feels like yesterday, but years ago I wrote a few articles about the "not-so-current" Front-end landscape and what to study to stay up to date:

- [What Front-end Developers need to know](/en/blog/what-front-end-developers-need-to-know/)
- [How to study, practice and land opportunities as a (Front-end) Developer](/en/blog/how-to-study-practice-and-land-opportunities-as-a-front-end-developer/)

I blinked and suddenly it's 2025!

And the most impressive part? Rereading those articles, I noticed that pretty much everything I said there is still valid, but with a few subtle changes that have much more to do with refining what we were already doing than with technological revolutions.

We went from a scenario (especially between 2014 and 2020) where a new lib popped up every week to a moment where the focus is much more on improving what we already have and doing it more efficiently.

## What changed in recent years?

In recent years, we've seen the main frameworks consolidate, like React (with Next.js, of course!), Angular and Vue, each with its own audience, but all aligned on the goal of making development more scalable and sustainable.

Back then there were still pretty heated discussions about [which was the best framework](/en/blog/react-angular-or-vue-which-javascript-framework-would-i-choose-today/). Today we can see that each one has its place and its advantages, and choosing between them is more a matter of preference and context than of "better" or "worse", since they're all quite mature and very similar in features.

At that point React was already the most popular framework, but there was no consolidation of patterns yet. Next.js, which was just starting to gain traction in 2020, is now practically omnipresent in React projects, bringing a series of features that make a developer's life easier, like SSR, SSG, API Routes and Image Optimization.

Vue has also evolved a lot, with version 3 bringing significant performance and scalability improvements, plus easier integration with other technologies, like Vite, which became an alternative to Webpack. The same goes for Angular, which kept evolving and helped boost technologies like Web Components and TypeScript.

With that we saw exponential growth in TypeScript usage, which was still "controversial" in 2020 but is now practically a requirement in more complex projects, bringing more safety and scalability to the code.

Front-end is more and more aligned with best practices in software architecture, testing and programming, things that were already well established in the Back-end. And that's great, because it shows that Front-end is no longer a "secondary" area and has established itself as a strategic and fundamental area for the success of any project.

We also saw a growing need for integration and DevOps knowledge, with the popularization of tools like Docker, Kubernetes and CI/CD, as well as platforms like Netlify and Vercel, which made developers' lives easier and allow faster and more efficient delivery.

## What's coming?

I think the future doesn't promise big revolutions, but rather changes that will make Front-end even more integrated with the business and more efficient.

### Automation and Artificial Intelligence

Low-code and no-code tools should gain traction, with increasingly powerful AI generating component code (and even entire applications!) automatically.

The thing is, devs' work won't be replaced by Artificial Intelligence, but devs who don't adapt to these tools will inevitably be replaced by people who make good use of them.

Remember that most of the work isn't exactly writing code, but analyzing, planning, making decisions, and of course, fixing bugs!

### Changes in the Role

The future of Front-end should be, more than ever, in integrating with other areas (not just other devs!) and acting as the interface between business, technology and user, translating customer needs into code but without necessarily having to write (as much) code to do it.

### Hard Skills

More generalist knowledge, like software architecture and technology integration, tends to be valued more than specializations in specific libraries, which can be replaced more easily.

Even though no big changes are expected in the technologies we use today, it's essential to keep a solid foundation in HTML, CSS and JavaScript, plus knowledge of React, Angular, Vue, TypeScript, testing and performance. It's worth highlighting that these technologies are converging more and more: frameworks like React, Angular and Vue keep incorporating each other's features, becoming progressively more alike.

With the development time of applications and components going down, the challenge becomes making sure these solutions are scalable and easy to maintain. In that context, software architecture, DevOps practices and good programming practices become even more relevant differentiators.

With that, mastery of agile methodologies and other ways of organizing projects and teams also tends to be valued more and more.

Soft skills, especially the ones related to communication, will play an increasingly decisive role in the success of frontenders.

When we talk about communication, it's not just about speaking well, but about knowing how to listen, understand and translate the customer's and the user's needs into code, and also knowing how to explain your decisions and defend your ideas.

Communication is an abstraction of your own thinking.

When you write code, you're communicating with the computer using a programming language and translating your needs into an algorithm. When you talk to a person, you're translating your ideas into words using natural language. With the popularization of AI tools, the communication you'll use with machines and with people is going to look more and more alike.

The better you know how to communicate, the better you'll be at translating your ideas into solutions that solve real problems.

## Conclusion

The changes tend to be more in how we do the things we already do than in the technologies themselves. In other words, it's possible that we'll automate the creation of components and applications more and more, but using tools that under the hood will generate code using the same technologies we already use.

That's why great communication, with enough abstraction ability to make good architecture decisions, is extremely relevant, as is DevOps knowledge, which is going to be more and more necessary. It's no longer so much about knowing a language's syntax by heart, but about knowing how to integrate technologies and people.

All of these changes are already happening gradually and the trend is for them to consolidate in the coming years. And remember that in the tech market, if you don't keep studying and updating yourself, you don't simply stay stagnant... you get worse!
