This is an absolutely personal text, packed with clichés.

Everything I'm going to mention is only about my own reality. People have different opportunities and paces, so don't feel bad if some of these things aren't part of your reality, if you can't put them into practice, or even if you disagree with something.

Some of these things I did in the past or do today, others I never put into practice and I think they could have brought important results to my career.

## 1. I would learn English

The mother of all clichés, present in 100% of the tips given by experts and also “experts”, from every profession. Still, it's impossible not to agree with how important it is and the impact it has in any profession.

English ended up becoming a common denominator among the many languages, and honestly, if I could go back in time, even before starting my career, this would be my focus.

The advantages are many:

- Books
- Articles
- Podcasts
- Discussions
- Opportunities abroad

I think it's possible to have a solid career (in Brazil) without fully mastering this language, despite the advantages listed above. The thing is that at some point having a good level of English starts making all the difference in reaching some career goals.

So I believe it's extremely important to learn English, but alongside development, because contact with the language becomes daily anyway when you work with programming.

In other words, if I could go back in time, I would combine my studies of programming languages with English.

## 2. I would learn programming logic

![[https://www.123rf.com/photo_16084282_abstract-word-cloud-for-logic-programming-with-related-tags-and-terms.html](https://www.123rf.com/photo_16084282_abstract-word-cloud-for-logic-programming-with-related-tags-and-terms.html)](assets/logica.png)_Credits:

Since I had no programming background at the start of my career, I miss having a more solid foundation in programming logic concepts, algorithms and computer science in general.

I'm sure my code would be much better today.

So this is another point that, along with English, I consider fundamental for any developer, and I wish I had found that out sooner.

## 3. I would follow the references of the profession

I would follow the people who make the most impact on the development community and are seen as professional or technical references.

Then I would try to understand why they're seen as references.

- What did they do?
- What did they create?
- What kind of professional attitude do they have?
- What do they eat?
- Where do they live?

And then I would get inspired to build my own story.

## 4. I would focus on the basic Front-end languages

![CSS, HTML and JavaScript](assets/css-html-js.png)

I've always worked with web development, so these languages are:

- HTML
- CSS
- JavaScript

As simple as that.

Of course, within these languages we have millions of libs, frameworks, pre-processors, for example:

### HTML

- Jade
- Pug
- Nunjucks
- Handlebars

### CSS

- Stylus
- Sass
- Less
- PostCSS
- CSS Modules
- Styled Components

### JavaScript

- React
- Angular
- Vue
- Svelte
- TypeScript

But under the hood, we've been using the same languages for many years, and we'll probably keep using them for quite a while.

So, before going deep on any lib, I would study this basic (and magical) trio a lot.

Still on this topic, I strongly recommend reading the article:
[What Front-end Developers need to know](/en/blog/what-front-end-developers-need-to-know/)
😁

## 5. I would learn one thing at a time

Being very practical, I would learn:

- Basic HTML, CSS and JavaScript (interface level)
- Then advanced JavaScript

First, because HTML and CSS naturally complement each other, and CSS especially is hella complex (write bad CSS and try to maintain it a year from now) and it's already enough content for a long time of study.

Second, because even though it doesn't make much sense these days, it's possible to build projects using ONLY HTML and CSS, without a fucking line of JavaScript. To prove it you can check out this project of mine from 2014, [CSS Components](https://css-components.felipefialho.com/).

Third, because the logic of CSS and JavaScript is completely different, and that's very confusing for beginners. Too much information, from such different languages, can be frustrating for someone starting out professionally.

So as soon as I was totally comfortable, I would dive headfirst into JavaScript, this language is powerful, amazing and has changed (and keeps changing) the world of development.

Take _soft skills_ to mean all your behavioral, emotional and social abilities.

Things like:

- Communication
- Teamwork
- Leadership
- Professionalism
- Flexibility
- Creativity
- Proactivity
- Empathy
- Etc

These skills are essential for any professional and are just as fundamental to career growth as technical aspects.

They can be developed, so I would definitely focus on developing these qualities from early on.

## 7. I would understand everything around my ecosystem

Development goes way beyond code.

So, to have an increasingly relevant professional life, I would seek to learn and understand from early on everything that surrounds my profession.

Thinking about Web Development, for example:

- Methodologies
- UX
- Design
- SEO
- Accessibility

And the job market itself and how companies expect to make money.

Believe it or not, all these things directly impact the quality of the code we write.

## 8. I would go to events

![MeetupCSS at Cubo](assets/meetup-css-2017.png)_MeetupCSS at Cubo_

I've written a few times about the
[importance of events for developers](/en/blog/why-every-developer-should-go-to-events/),
so I won't dwell on this subject.

I'd say smaller events like Meetups are even more interesting for people who are just starting out, they're usually free and there's more closeness between people.

If there were no event in the city where I live or on the subject I was studying, I would create my own event. Two people are already enough for a Meetup to happen 😉

Unfortunately I found out late how impactful events can be for a career, and I would definitely have started going as soon as my career as a developer began.

## 9. I would listen to more experienced professionals

People with many years in the profession have surely been through many situations that could be useful to me.

I wouldn't hesitate to learn from and absorb as much as possible from more seasoned professionals, things like:

- Code patterns and tips
- Professional decisions and options

## 10. But I would have my own opinion

![https://www.pensador.com/frase/MTYzNzA](assets/raul-2017.png)_pensador.com_

A counterpoint to the previous tip.

It's super important to learn from other people's mistakes and wins, it helps you grow professionally, but those mistakes and wins are still... other people's.

I would try to absorb everything more experienced professionals told me, but I would never take it as absolute truth.

I would always form my own opinions based on things I lived through, not on things I heard someone say.

## 11. I would avoid doing freelance gigs

Bernard De Luna and Zeno Rocha
[talked about this](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=y8UUKv7j0l0) in 2012 and I still agree.

Obviously this tip doesn't apply to those who make a living from freelancing.

And to be clear, as I said at the beginning of the text, people live in different realities, so this may make sense to me but maybe not to you. There's nothing wrong with that.

When you freelance, many times you'll get paid much less than you should and work much more than you should. Meanwhile, the person paying you will think they're paying you way more than you deserve.

So, unless I needed extra income at the end of the month A LOT, I would avoid freelancing so I could focus fully on my studies, going to events and working on open source projects.

## 12. I would take care of my mental health

A
[serious problem](https://medium.com/@bernarddeluna/ol%C3%A1-devs-precisamos-falar-sobre-estresse-e-ansiedade-bde1b04f897e),
that hits a huge number of developers, and can lead to
[demotivation](/blog/a-motivacao-desmotivacional-no-mundo-do-desenvolvimento/) (in Portuguese).

We're bombarded with a huge amount of information every day, new libs, new frameworks, new methodologies... it's almost impossible to absorb all that knowledge.

I've been hit by this problem several times, especially during the most intense periods of study, which is a contradiction: the more you study and learn, the more you find out you're far from reaching a state where you consider yourself a good professional.

It's very hard to deal with, but here's what I would do to protect myself from these problems:

- Set short-term personal goals
- Not compare myself to other people
- Learn one thing at a time
- Have hobbies that have nothing to do with work
- Do physical activities
- Avoid working or studying on weekends

## 13. I would do physical activities

I consider this another absolutely crucial point, after all, people who work with programming tend to spend many hours in front of the computer.

There are many kinds of physical activity and you can pick your favorite or whatever best fits your day-to-day, the most important thing is that it's frequent.

- Gym
- Running
- Dancing
- Swimming
- Soccer
- Etc

You can also start slow, fitting the activity into your day, with things like:

- Walking to work
- Getting off a few stops or stations early
- Avoiding elevators
- Etc

Other things like better eating, increased focus and especially building better habits tend to come as a bonus when you start doing physical activities.

Besides improving your health, the result will definitely also show in the quality of your work.

## 14. I wouldn't give up my hobbies

![Sacred Sunday soccer](assets/futebol.png)_Sacred Sunday soccer_

It's natural (unfortunately), when you're focused on studies and professional life, for activities you loved doing and that kept you excited your whole life to take a back seat.

But that shouldn't happen.

So I would always reserve space in my schedule to do all the activities I like and that make me feel good.

I wouldn't give that up, it does the mind a lot of good, directly impacting tip number 12.

That also means I would rarely study or work on weekends, doing everything I could to spend that time well with people and things that made me happy.
